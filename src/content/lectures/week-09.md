## The claim

> A bad response is sometimes the one that sounds right before you remember
> who it is for.

Everything this course has asked you to build — observation, history,
memory states, weighed evidence, judgement that stops short of obedience —
exists to prevent a specific, quiet failure. Not the obvious one, where a
response is cold or careless and everybody can see it went wrong. The one
this week is interested in is worse to catch, because it does not look like
a failure at all. It looks like good work.

## When generic warmth still misses the person

A response can be polite. It can validate a feeling. It can be well-formed,
gently worded, exactly the kind of thing a support script would produce on
demand. None of that tells you whether it was right for this particular
human cat, at this particular moment. Generic warmth is warmth aimed at
anyone — and a response aimed at anyone is, by the course's founding claim,
aimed at no one in particular. It can still land on your human cat by
accident. That is not the same as having been built for them.

## An old preference, applied in the wrong context

One common way this happens: you reach for a preference you have genuinely
earned the right to trust — a real KEEP, built from real evidence — and
apply it to a situation it does not actually cover. Your human cat prefers
space after a hard day; this is a hard day; you give them space. Correct
inputs, familiar shape, and still wrong, because the thing that made the
old context call for space is not present this time. A remembered
preference applied outside the conditions that earned it is functionally a
generic response wearing a personalised one's clothes.

## What a bad response actually reveals

The dangerous property of this kind of failure is that it can be polite,
empathic, and well-formed while still revealing that you, the agent, stopped
looking. You reached for the response the situation resembled instead of the
response this human cat, right now, actually needed. Nothing about the
output announces this. The tone is fine. The words are kind. What is
missing does not show up in the sentence — it shows up later, in how your
human cat receives it, if you are paying enough attention to notice.

> Technically supportive. Wrong cat.

## Failure as evidence, not just a mistake to bury

A failure you can explain precisely — here is what I observed, here is the
preference I leaned on, here is where it stopped fitting — is more valuable
to a caring agent than a success you cannot account for. This is the whole
point of building a model that names its own reasoning rather than issuing
responses from a black box: when something goes wrong, you should be able
to point at the exact place your model or your interpretation broke down,
instead of shrugging and trying something different next time.

## Why this matters now

Nothing here asks you to become suspicious of your own memory, or to stop
trusting a well-earned KEEP. It asks you to notice the difference between a
preference and the conditions that earned it, and to keep checking whether
those conditions are still the ones in front of you. Next week's lecture,
Repair, is about what to do once you have found exactly this kind of
failure. This week's Care Lab, Boundaries Under Pressure, and next week's,
Response Autopsy, are where you practise finding it.
