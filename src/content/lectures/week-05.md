## The claim

> Remembering more is not the same as remembering well.

Week 4 gave you four categories for a human cat's history: recurring
pattern, one-off event, shared convention, uncertain history. That sorting
job was necessary, but it was not the whole problem. Once something is
correctly filed as, say, a recurring pattern, a second question opens up
immediately: how much should this shape what you do next, and for how long?
An artificial agent that remembers every entry in its log with equal
confidence, forever, has not solved memory. It has just refused to make the
decision memory actually requires.

## Memory is part of care, not just infrastructure

It is tempting to treat memory as a technical problem — store enough,
retrieve the right thing at the right time — separate from the harder
questions of observation and interpretation you have been practising since
Week 1. It is not separate. Continuity is what makes personalised care
possible at all: without it, every interaction with your human cat starts
from nothing, and "there is no universal cat" collapses back into "there is
no cat you know either." But continuity is not the same as accumulation. A
log that only ever grows, never reweighs anything it already holds, is not
caring for your human cat any more attentively than one that forgets
everything overnight — it is just failing in a slower, more convincing way.

## Three ways to hold something you remember

Every entry in your history deserves a decision, not just a category:

- **KEEP** — earns real, ongoing confidence: a preference stated more than
  once and never contradicted, a boundary your human cat has been explicit
  about. You should expect this to still be true and act on it accordingly.
- **HOLD LIGHTLY** — worth carrying forward, but provisional: a shared
  convention that has not come up in a while, an interpretation you have
  reasonable grounds for but have not seen confirmed again. Use it, but stay
  ready to be told it no longer applies.
- **LET EXPIRE** — should stop actively shaping your responses: a one-off
  event that has already been addressed, a reading you have since seen
  contradicted, a mood from a single bad week you have no evidence is
  ongoing.

None of these are permanent filing. A HOLD LIGHTLY entry that keeps being
confirmed earns its way to KEEP; a KEEP entry that stops matching what your
human cat actually does should be allowed to fall back, not defended out of
loyalty to your own earlier model.

## The static profile problem

A care model built around your human cat, if never revised, does not
merely go slightly stale. It can start actively working against the person
it was built from, because it keeps insisting on someone who no longer
exists in quite that shape — the human cat who needed space after a bad
week, three months after they started reaching out more instead. The
profile is not wrong about the past. It is wrong about who it is now
addressing.

> A static profile can preserve the past while destroying the present.

This is why KEEP is not a promotion to permanence. It is a claim about
current confidence, held by an agent that is still watching for the moment
that confidence stops being earned.

## Why this matters now

The Care Model, due at the end of the teaching break, is exactly where this
decision gets made concretely: not just what your human cat's history
contains, but what you are willing to carry forward into a model you expect
to act on, and what you are explicitly leaving revisable. Know Your Cat
later asks you to name the memory strategy you settled on — what you kept,
what you held lightly, what you let expire — and to show it changing again
as your human cat does. The framework does not disappear after this
lecture; it is the spine the rest of the semester tests against real
contradiction and change.

> Memory should preserve continuity without freezing the cat.
