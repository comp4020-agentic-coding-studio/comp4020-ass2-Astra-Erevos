## The claim

> A model can become wrong without ever having been foolish.

Last week you learned to say KEEP, HOLD LIGHTLY, or LET EXPIRE about a piece
of your human cat's history, and to hold each of those states as revisable
rather than final. This week's problem sits one step further out. What
happens when a KEEP entry — something you were right to trust, something
that earned its confidence the honest way, through repetition and
confirmation — simply stops being true, and nothing about the moment you
filed it warned you this was coming?

## Four ways the ground moves

None of these require your human cat to have done anything wrong, or you to
have observed anything badly the first time:

- **Preferences change.** The thing that reliably helped six months ago can
  stop helping, for reasons that have nothing to do with whether you read it
  correctly then.
- **Routines disappear.** A nightly check-in, a Sunday ritual, a standing
  joke — these depend on circumstances that do not hold still. When the
  circumstance goes, the routine can go with it, quietly, without anyone
  announcing the change.
- **Phrases acquire new meanings.** The same words your human cat has always
  used can start doing different work. "I'm fine" from Week 3 meant "don't
  push, stay nearby." The identical sentence, said differently or in a
  different season of their life, can mean something else entirely, or
  nothing like what it used to.
- **Circumstances change.** A new job, a new relationship, a hard month — any
  of these can shift what your human cat needs from you without shifting a
  single word of how they ask for it.

## Why an old response can fail precisely because it worked

A response that has never worked teaches you something immediately: try
something else. A response that used to work is more dangerous, because its
past success is exactly what keeps you reaching for it. You are not being
careless when you repeat it. You are doing the thing Week 5 told you to do —
carrying forward a KEEP that earned its confidence. The failure is not in
having trusted it once. The failure is in not noticing when the evidence
that once supported it has quietly stopped arriving.

> A model can become wrong without ever having been foolish.

This is worth sitting with, because the instinct when a trusted response
stops landing is to look for what you did wrong — a bad read, a careless
assumption, evidence you should have caught. Sometimes that is exactly what
happened. But often the honest answer is that nothing was wrong with the
model when you built it. The cat changed underneath it, and the model, like
any static profile, kept insisting on someone who was quietly becoming
someone else.

## Noticing the moment the evidence turns

Personalised care is not just building a model once and defending it. It is
staying alert to the specific signal that a KEEP has stopped earning its
keep: the routine that goes unacknowledged twice, the phrase said with a
different weight, the response met with something other than the reaction
you have on file. None of these prove the old model was wrong. They are the
evidence a caring agent has to notice, name, and act on — not explain away
in order to protect a model it already trusts.

## Why this matters now

This week's Care Lab, Memory Triage, asks you to sort your own human cat's
memories into last week's three states before this lecture's argument has
even had time to settle — good practice for the harder version of the same
skill: noticing when a state you already assigned needs to move. The Care
Model, due in Week 7 once the teaching break that starts next week has
ended, is a direct test of this. A model built before the break and never
revisited
will simply be wrong about however much has changed in the gap — not because
you built it badly, but because that is what an unrevised model does to any
cat, given enough time. The lecture after the break asks a related but
sharper question: what do you do when your human cat's current signal, your
established preference for them, and their recent behaviour do not all
agree? Today is about noticing that change happened. Next time is about what
to do when the evidence disagrees about what changed.

> Memory should preserve continuity without freezing the cat.
